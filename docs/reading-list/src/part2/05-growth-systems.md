# 5 · Growth & Stock-Picking Systems

> Part II · Chapter 5 · Books: 1 · Topic level: company ▸ micro

## What this topic is about

Every book so far has asked *what is a company worth?* This chapter asks a
different question: **what do the biggest stock-market winners of history look
like *before* they win?** It is the growth investor's answer to the value lens of
[Chapter 4](04-fundamentals-valuation.md) — same financial statements, opposite
pricing instinct: instead of paying a fair price for a great business, buy
*faster-than-average growth* while it is still being recognised by the market.

This is a single-book chapter, and deliberately so. *How to Make Money in Stocks*
by William J. O'Neil is one of the best-selling stock-picking books ever written,
and it is the only title the collection needs to teach the **CANSLIM** system in
full. O'Neil founded *Investor's Business Daily*, and his claim is what makes the
book mandatory here: CANSLIM was reverse-engineered from the actual biggest
winning stocks of the twentieth century (he studied every major market winner
since the 1950s), not invented as a theory first. It is a checklist of traits
that winning stocks *shared at the moment they began their runs*.

The book's position in the curriculum matters as much as its content. It reads
the fundamentals like [Chapter 4](04-fundamentals-valuation.md) — current and
annual earnings growth, the quality of leadership — but it *acts* on charts: buy
points, base patterns, volume, and the market's own direction. That makes it the
structural bridge from the investing half of the book into the technical-analysis
half that begins in [Chapter 6](../part3/06-trends-dow-theory.md). If Part II
teaches you *which* companies deserve your money, this chapter is where you learn
*when* the market itself agrees to pay up.

## Study synthesis — the CANSLIM system

Because this is a single-book chapter, the synthesis *is* the system. CANSLIM is
an acronym whose seven letters are seven independent screens, and O'Neil's point
is that a stock should satisfy **all seven** — one strong letter does not carry a
weak stock. In full:

```text
C — Current quarterly earnings   EPS up sharply vs the same quarter last year
                                  (target: +20–25% or more)
A — Annual earnings growth        Consistent EPS growth for each of the last 3–5
                                  years (target: ~25%+); sales growth too
N — Newness                        New products, new management, new highs —
                                  stocks near all-time highs are strengths
S — Supply and demand             Smaller share count = tighter supply; few
                                  shares outstanding, rising volume on price up
L — Leader or laggard             Own the #1 or #2 stock in its group, not #8
I — Institutional sponsorship     Owned and being accumulated by quality
                                  institutions (mutual/pension funds)
M — Market direction              The market's own trend overrides individual
                                  picks — about 3 of every 4 stocks fall in a
                                  declining market
```

**1. Earnings are the engine (C and A).** O'Neil is unapologetically a growth
buyer: accelerating current-quarter EPS (C) plus a multi-year record of 25%-plus
annual growth (A) are his non-negotiables. He is also an innovator for his era in
demanding *sales* growth alongside earnings — a company growing profits by cost
cutting alone rarely makes the winner list.

**2. Newness is a feature, not a risk (N).** The hardest mental shift in the
book: O'Neil argues the public misreads *new highs* as expensive when they are
actually the single most reliable common trait of major winners. New products,
new management, new industry conditions — and a price making new highs — signal
that the market is repricing the company.

**3. Supply is part of demand (S).** A company with relatively few shares
outstanding needs less buying pressure to move — so tight share count and
buyback-friendly companies are structurally favoured, while heavy new-share
issuance dilutes a rally.

**4. Leaders, not laggards (L).** Buy the top one or two names in the strongest
industry groups. Relative price strength — the leader's price holding up while
laggards fade — is the screen O'Neil uses to tell them apart.

**5. Institutions move prices (I).** Mutual funds and pension funds are the
market's biggest buyers; a stock that quality institutions own — and are adding
to — has the fuel behind it that a retail-only favourite lacks.

**6. The market is the boss (M).** O'Neil's bluntest claim: in a market
correction, roughly three out of four stocks fall regardless of quality. CANSLIM
is only ever "on" when the broad market averages are in an uptrend; when they are
not, the disciplined response is cash, not courage.

**7. The chart supplies the buy point.** The system is completed by O'Neil's
chart work: the **cup-with-handle** (a rounded decline and recovery, with the
handle breaking out on strong volume), the **double bottom** (a W-shaped base), the
**flat base**, and the **pivot point** — the price at which the stock breaks out
of its base. Buying at or near the pivot, on volume at least 40–50% above
average, is the entry discipline.

**8. Two hard risk rules.** O'Neil's risk management is famous because it is
mechanical. **The 7–8% rule:** sell any stock that falls 7–8% below your
purchase price — no averaging down, no rationalisation, no exceptions for "good
companies." **The 20–25% rule:** sell into strength when a stock gains 20–25%
and take the profit, then redeploy into a fresh setup. The two rules together
cap both the loss side and the give-back side of every position.

**9. Why this is the bridge to Part III.** Every letter of CANSLIM except M is a
fundamental screen — and yet nothing is bought or sold without a chart. M reads
the market; N reads the price level; the pivot and the bases read price action
and volume. The book is the curriculum's first sustained proof that the best
investors and the best technicians are describing the *same* stock, just with
different tools — which is exactly what [Chapter 6](../part3/06-trends-dow-theory.md)
and the chart chapters that follow assume.

## Book-by-book annotations

### How to Make Money in Stocks — William J. O'Neil (2009, ★ 4.3)

O'Neil is the founder of *Investor's Business Daily*, and this is his complete
growth-investing method in one book: the CANSLIM screens above, the chart
patterns that locate buy points, the volume-confirmation rules, and the two
mechanical risk rules. The fourth edition (2009) is the edition in this
collection — it carries the lessons of the 2000–2002 bear market, a long section
on understanding the market's own moves, and the case studies of market winners
that O'Neil uses to prove the system was reverse-engineered from real stocks, not
invented on paper.

- **Read it for:** the complete CANSLIM checklist (each letter gets its own
  chapter); cup-with-handle, double-bottom, flat-base, and pivot-point chart
  work; the 7–8% loss and 20–25% profit rules as a coherent risk system; the
  market-direction (M) chapter and the general-market correction rules; the
  historical winner case studies.
- **Model refresh:** newer edition: "Complete" ed., 2013; ask your AI study
  companion to expand these notes from its training data.

## Reading order

A single-book chapter, so the order is internal:

1. **Read the CANSLIM letters first** (Chapters 1–11) — the fundamental screens
   are the base.
2. **Then the chart chapters** — bases, buy points, and volume.
3. **Then the market-direction chapters** — because M gates everything.
4. **Read the risk rules twice** — the 7–8% and 20–25% rules are the part
   readers skip and the part that keeps you alive for the next setup.

## Self-test questions

1. Write out CANSLIM letter by letter, from memory.
2. What is the difference between C (current quarterly earnings) and A (annual
   earnings growth) — and what does O'Neil demand of each?
3. Why does O'Neil treat a stock making new price highs as a strength rather
   than a danger? What is the psychological error most investors make instead?
4. Sketch a cup-with-handle base and mark where the buy point is. What should
   volume be doing at the breakout?
5. State the 7–8% rule exactly. What does O'Neil say about averaging down?
6. State the 20–25% rule. What is the disciplined action after the sale?
7. Which letter of CANSLIM is about the whole market rather than the individual
   stock, and why does O'Neil insist it overrides the other six?
8. Why is this book the bridge between Part II (investing) and Part III
   (technical analysis)? Name one fundamental screen and one chart signal that
   appear in the same decision.
