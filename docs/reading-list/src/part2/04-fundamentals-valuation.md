# 4 · Financial Statements, Ratios & Valuation

> Part II · Chapter 4 · Books: 3 · Topic level: macro ▸ company ▸ micro

## What this topic is about

[Chapter 3](../part1/03-allocation-indexing.md) made the case that a low-cost index of
the whole market is the hard benchmark to beat. This chapter is for the reader
who, having accepted that challenge, now wants to look *inside one company* and
answer the two questions that all stock-picking reduces to: **what is this
business actually earning, and how much should I pay for that earning power?**

This is the "company" level of the macro→micro path. The market (Part I) told you
how prices behave; the company tells you what a price *represents*. The books
here teach a shared vocabulary — the three financial statements, the margin and
return ratios, and valuation multiples — that every later chapter quietly
assumes. If a future chapter says "the company trades at 30× earnings," this is
where that sentence gets decoded.

The three books approach the company from three directions. *The Five Rules for
Successful Stock Investing* is the classic: how to judge a business's economics
before you ever look at its price. *Fundamental Analysis For Dummies* is the
from-zero on-ramp: what a balance sheet is, line by line, and how to run the
ratios yourself. *Fundamental Analysis and Position Trading* is the bridge out of
this chapter: having found a fundamentally sound company, how to combine that
analysis with chart-based timing to trade positions rather than just "invest."
Reading all three in sequence moves you from *understanding* a company to
*acting* on a company.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. The three statements are one story, told three ways.**
The income statement reports profit over a period; the balance sheet reports what
the company owns and owes at one moment; the cash flow statement reports the
actual cash moving in and out. The shared lesson of all three books: **earnings
can be managed, cash is hard to fake.** The cash flow statement is the
cross-check on everything the income statement claims.

**2. The economic moat is the only thing that protects a profit.**
Dorsey's framework sorts companies into **wide-moat**, **narrow-moat**, and
**no-moat** by asking one question: *if a competitor wanted your profit, how hard
would it be to take it?* The five moat sources:

- **Intangible assets** — brands, patents, licences that competitors cannot copy.
- **Switching costs** — the pain a customer pays to move to a rival.
- **Network effect** — the product gets more valuable as more people use it.
- **Cost advantage** — structurally lower cost per unit (process, location, scale).
- **Efficient scale** — a market too small to profitably support a second entrant.

**3. Margins and returns on capital measure how good the moat is.**
Wide-moat economics show up as consistently high margins and high returns on the
capital the business employs. One period of high profit is luck; ten years of
high return on capital is a moat. Krantz teaches how to compute them; Dorsey
teaches why they matter more than growth.

**4. The ratio kit, in one place.**
Every later chapter references these; learn them now.

```text
P/E            = price per share / earnings per share        (how much you pay per ₹1 of profit)
P/B            = price per share / book value per share
P/S            = market cap / total sales
EV/EBITDA      = enterprise value / EBITDA
                   where EV = market cap + debt − cash
ROE            = net income / shareholders' equity
ROCE           = operating profit / capital employed (equity + debt)
D/E            = total debt / shareholders' equity
Current ratio  = current assets / current liabilities
```

**5. Moat plus valuation beats moat alone.**
The chapter's strongest shared warning: a great company at a crazy price is a
*bad investment*. Dorsey's rule is to pay a *fair* price for a *wide-moat*
company — moat tells you the business is good, the ratios tell you the *price* is
good, and you need both. This is also where indexing keeps its dignity: if you
cannot find moat *and* fair price, Chapter 3's index fund is the honest fallback.

**6. Fundamentals choose the what; the chart chooses the when.**
Bulkowski is the transition book of this chapter: fundamentals identify the
company worth owning, position trading supplies the entry, exit, and stop-loss
timing. That pairing — *what to own, then when to buy* — is exactly the structure
of [Chapter 5](05-growth-systems.md) and the whole of Part III.

## Book-by-book annotations

### The Five Rules for Successful Stock Investing — Pat Dorsey (2004, ★ 4.6)

The classic in this chapter. Dorsey, Morningstar's director of equity research,
turns the firm's internal stock-research method into a book: find the business
with a durable advantage, verify the advantage in the financial statements, and
buy only at a fair price. His "five rules" — do adequate research before buying;
understand the business's economics; buy only at a fair price; hold while the
business thesis holds; and know when to sell — read like a discipline checklist
rather than a forecasting system, which is precisely the point.

- **Read it for:** the wide/narrow/no-moat framework; the five moat sources; how
  to read statements the Morningstar way (including the fictional "Hott &
  Co." and "Sully Industries" worked examples); the sell-and-admit-mistakes
  discipline.
- **Model refresh:** edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Fundamental Analysis For Dummies — Matt Krantz (2009, ★ 4.3)

The from-zero statements course. Written by a USA Today financial-markets
reporter, it walks a complete beginner through each of the three statements line
by line, defines every ratio, and shows the arithmetic with worked numbers — no
prior accounting knowledge assumed. Where Dorsey assumes you can read a 10-K,
Krantz assumes you cannot yet, and fixes that in two hundred pages.

- **Read it for:** statement-by-statement walkthroughs; ratio definitions and
  worked calculations; how news and corporate events move the fundamentals;
  the "common mistakes" chapters (why a single quarter's jump can mislead).
- **Model refresh:** newer edition: 2nd ed., 2016 (suspected); ask your AI study
  companion to expand these notes from its training data.

### Fundamental Analysis and Position Trading (Evolution of a Trader) — Thomas N. Bulkowski (2013, ★ —)

*Note: no rating is recorded for this title — it is not part of the 126-title
file collection and was added to this chapter on request.*

Bulkowski is best known in this library for his chart-pattern encyclopedias; this
volume is the one where he turns from charts to fundamentals. Written as the
"fundamental" leg of his *Evolution of a Trader* series, it teaches you to score
a company's financial health and then — the part that makes it unique here —
explicitly pairs that analysis with position-trading rules: chart-based entry,
stop placement, and exit timing. It is the only book in this chapter that treats
fundamentals as a *trade setup* rather than an *investor's verdict*.

- **Read it for:** a practical, scored approach to statement analysis; how to
  rank candidate companies on financial strength; the explicit
  fundamentals-plus-charts workflow (analysis, then entry timing, then stops);
  the bridge from this chapter to Part III's chart chapters.
- **Model refresh:** edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

## Reading order

1. **Fundamental Analysis For Dummies** — the statements from zero (skip this only
   if you can already read a balance sheet).
2. **The Five Rules for Successful Stock Investing** — the moat framework and the
   valuation discipline (the heart of the chapter).
3. **Fundamental Analysis and Position Trading** — the trading overlay, best read
   right before moving on to Part III.

## Self-test questions

1. What is the difference between the income statement and the cash flow
   statement — and why do analysts say the cash flow statement "lies less"?
2. Define "economic moat" in your own words, then name the five moat sources and
   give one real-world example of each.
3. What does ROE measure, and why can high debt inflate it misleadingly?
4. Why is "great company" alone *not* a reason to buy? What does the price add?
5. What is the difference between P/E and EV/EBITDA — what does each try to
   measure that the other misses?
6. Compute D/E and the current ratio from a balance sheet you have at hand. What
   would each number have to look like before it worries you?
7. In Bulkowski's framework, which decision belongs to fundamentals and which to
   the chart? Why can't the chart answer "what to own"?
