# 2 · The Investing Masters: Value & Growth

> Part I · Chapter 2 · Books: 6 · Topic level: macro ▸ micro

## What this topic is about

[Chapter 1](01-how-markets-work.md) asked what the market is. This chapter asks the
question everything else in this book depends on: **is there a disciplined way to
buy a business for less than it is worth?** The six books here are the founding
canon of active equity investing — the writers whose names became the disciplines
they invented. Benjamin Graham gave us value investing and the margin of safety.
Philip Fisher gave us growth investing and a 15-point checklist for quality.
Warren Buffett, Graham's student and Fisher's reader, fused the two into the most
documented track record in finance. Peter Lynch showed the same habits work with
ordinary, everyday knowledge. Robert Hagstrom and Lawrence Cunningham are the
explainers of Buffett; Daniel Pecaut and Corey Wrenn recorded him live, year
after year, at Berkshire's shareholder meetings.

This is the *philosophy* chapter: it builds a worldview before any spreadsheet.
The books assume you can read a balance sheet (that machinery arrives in
[Chapter 4](../part2/04-fundamentals-valuation.md)); here the subject is how to
*think* about a business and its price, not how to compute one. If Chapter 1 is
the macro lens on markets, this chapter is the macro lens on the investor
himself.

The central idea uniting all six books is Buffett's: **price is what you pay,
value is what you get.** The gap between the two is where investing happens. But
the gap is visible only to someone who can estimate value — which is why the
second uniting idea is temperament. Graham's warning sets the tone: the
investor's chief problem — and his worst enemy — is likely to be himself.
Mr. Market, the margin of safety, intrinsic value, and the circle of competence
are the shared vocabulary you will use for the rest of this book.

The framework is broker- and exchange-agnostic. Nothing here requires NYSE
listings: the same quality checks and safety disciplines apply to NSE/BSE
stocks, with the extra caution that corporate-governance and data-quality risk
is exactly what Fisher's checklist is built to probe.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. Mr. Market is a manic business partner, not a price feed.**
Graham's parable: you own a stake in a private business, and every day a manic
partner named Mr. Market offers to buy your stake or sell you his, at whatever
price his mood dictates. Some days he is euphoric, some days depressed — but the
price he quotes is *his* price, not the business's. You are free to ignore him,
or to exploit him. The lesson: the market exists to serve you, not to instruct
you. Daily prices are quotes to use, never verdicts on the business.

**2. The margin of safety is the whole game.**
Buy below a conservative estimate of value so that being wrong — or unlucky —
does not cost you. Graham made it the defining requirement of an investment
operation, alongside thorough analysis and an adequate return. Because every
valuation is an estimate, the margin of safety is not a bonus; it is the
insurance that keeps honest mistakes survivable.

**3. Intrinsic value is an estimate, not a print.**
Buffett's working definition (in his letters, collected in *The Essays of Warren
Buffett*): intrinsic value is the discounted value of the cash a business will
generate over its remaining life. Note the word *estimate*: two honest investors
can disagree on a fair value for the same company. That is precisely why the
margin of safety exists, and why no book here promises a formula that prints
"fair value." The closest any of them comes to arithmetic is Graham's net-net
idea (price below working capital) and Greenblatt's two-factor screen — the
subject of [Chapter 3](03-allocation-indexing.md).

**4. Value and growth are joined at the hip.**
The retail version of this debate is a war: value investors buy cheap,
beaten-down stocks; growth investors buy expensive, exciting ones. The books
deny the split. Graham's later thinking concedes that growth can be a legitimate
component of a value computation; Fisher's entire method is buying a great
company for what it will become; Buffett states it flatly — growth is one
component of value, and value is always the object. The real dividing line is
*paying less than value* versus *paying more than value*, not the labels "value"
and "growth."

**5. The circle of competence.**
Buffett's rule: define the perimeter of what you genuinely understand, and stay
inside it. The circle is not a fixed list of industries — it is a boundary drawn
by your own knowledge, and knowing its edge is what prevents catastrophe.
Lynch's "invest in what you know" is the same idea applied to everyday life,
with the caveat that knowing a product is only the starting point of research,
never the whole of it.

**6. Fisher's 15-point checklist is how you inspect quality.**
The checklist asks fifteen questions about the company — market potential, R&D
effectiveness, sales organization, profit margins, cost analysis, earnings
quality, management depth, and more — that separate a genuinely superior
business from a merely popular one. Its companion, the "scuttlebutt" technique,
is fieldwork: talking to employees, suppliers, customers, and competitors.
Together they turn "I like this company" into "I verified this company," and they
are the ancestor of the quality screens you will meet in Part II.

**7. Buffett's four filters: quality, growth, and price.**
Hagstrom distills Buffett's approach into four filters: (1) *the business* —
understandable, with durable economics and a moat; (2) *management* — rational,
candid, and honest with shareholders; (3) *financials* — high returns on
capital, growing earnings, manageable debt; (4) *value* — a price that leaves a
margin of safety. The first three filters are quality-and-growth; only the
fourth is price. Buffett's compressed version of the whole system: it is far
better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a
wonderful price.

**8. Read Buffett through his own letters.**
*The Essays of Warren Buffett* organizes the shareholder letters by theme —
governance, finance and investing, common stocks, mergers, accounting — so you
can study how Buffett reasons, not just what he bought. *University of Berkshire
Hathaway* adds thirty years of live Q&A with Buffett and Munger, showing the
philosophy under pressure. The letters are the primary source: they show a
decision *process*, which is the only thing from this chapter you can actually
take with you.

## Book-by-book annotations

### One Up On Wall Street — Peter Lynch (2000, ★ 4.5)

Lynch's argument is that a professional money manager's biggest rival is an
ordinary person paying attention to ordinary life: the shopper, the commuter,
the person who notices that every store in town is using a particular product.
He divides stocks into six categories — slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers,
cyclicals, turnarounds, and asset plays — and teaches that each behaves
differently and demands different rules. The famous output is the "tenbagger," a
stock that rises tenfold, and the companion discipline that research begins with
what you already understand.

- **Read it for:** the six categories and how each one should be treated; the
  tenbagger search; the PEG-style habit of checking growth against price; the
  honest version of "invest in what you know."

### Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits — Philip A. Fisher (2003, ★ 4.3)

The growth-stock classic and the book that converted Buffett from pure Graham to
quality-growth. Fisher's thesis: buy a handful of genuinely superior companies —
strong products, strong R&D, honest and deep management, high margins — and hold
them for the long term, ignoring the market's noise. His two tools are the
15-point checklist for identifying such companies and the "scuttlebutt"
technique for verifying them through first-hand sources.

- **Read it for:** the 15-point checklist, used as an actual checklist; the
  scuttlebutt method of fieldwork; why a few well-researched holdings beat a
  crowded portfolio; the long-horizon patience that growth investing demands.

  expand these notes from its training data.

### The Warren Buffett Way — Robert G. Hagstrom (2005, ★ 4.0)

The first systematic study of *how* Buffett invests, built from real purchases —
GEICO, See's Candies, the Washington Post, Coca-Cola — and organized into the
four filters: business, management, financials, and value. Hagstrom's
contribution is making Buffett's scattered remarks into a repeatable process,
and naming the "economic moat" — the durable competitive advantage that lets a
wonderful business stay wonderful.

- **Read it for:** the four filters as an actual decision process; the moat /
  economic-franchise concept; the case studies of real Buffett purchases; the
  explicit links back to Graham's intrinsic value and margin of safety.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 3rd ed., 2013; ask your AI study companion
  to expand these notes from its training data.

### The Intelligent Investor — Benjamin Graham (2006, ★ 4.5)

The foundational text of value investing, first published in 1949 and still the
book Buffett recommends before his own. Graham defines an investment operation,
explains the Mr. Market parable, and makes the margin of safety the hinge of the
entire method. He divides readers into defensive and enterprising investors,
gives each a portfolio policy, and insists that the market's daily opinion is a
service to be used, never obeyed. The 2006 edition carries Jason Zweig's running
commentary, which translates Graham's examples for modern markets.

- **Read it for:** the definition of an investment operation; Chapter 8
  (Mr. Market) and Chapter 20 (margin of safety) — the two chapters Buffett
  calls the heart of the book; the defensive investor's allocation rules;
  Zweig's commentary as a bridge to today's data.

  expand these notes from its training data.

### The Essays of Warren Buffett — ed. Lawrence A. Cunningham (2015, ★ 4.7)

Buffett's own words, organized: Cunningham rearranged decades of Berkshire
Hathaway shareholder letters by theme rather than chronology, so that
governance, corporate finance, investing alternatives, common stocks, mergers,
and accounting each form a coherent essay. This is the primary source of the
chapter's synthesis — the circle of competence, the owner-earnings discussion,
the moat language, and the "price is what you pay" line all live here in
Buffett's own prose.

- **Read it for:** Buffett on intrinsic value and owner earnings in his own
  words; the governance essays on honest management; the accounting chapters
  that show how to distrust reported numbers; the raw letters that underlie
  every summary in this chapter.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 5th ed., 2020 (suspected); ask your AI study
  companion to expand these notes from its training data.

### University of Berkshire Hathaway — Daniel Pecaut & Corey Wrenn (2017, ★ 4.6)

Thirty years of notes from Berkshire Hathaway's annual shareholder meetings
(1986–2015), where Buffett and Charlie Munger answer real questions live.
Because the book is chronological, you watch the same philosophy tested across
bull markets, crashes, and new industries. Munger's contribution gets equal
space — mental models, incentives, and the refusal of complexity — which makes
this the best single volume for seeing the philosophy as a *lived* process
rather than a theory.

- **Read it for:** the live Q&A format — real questions, real reasoning; the
  multi-decade arc of a philosophy that never changed shape; Munger's
  frameworks alongside Buffett's; a patient, high-volume way to build judgment.

## Reading order

1. **One Up On Wall Street** — the friendly on-ramp; proves value thinking needs
   no Wall Street background.
2. **The Intelligent Investor** — the core text; at minimum read Chapters 8 and
   20 carefully.
3. **Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits** — the growth half of the synthesis.
4. **The Warren Buffett Way** — the merger of value and growth into a single
   process.
5. **The Essays of Warren Buffett** — the primary source, once you can follow a
   letter.
6. **University of Berkshire Hathaway** — the live record; best saved for depth
   once the concepts are familiar.

## Self-test questions

1. Restate the Mr. Market parable in your own words. What is the market for —
   information or prices?
2. Define the margin of safety and explain why it matters *precisely because*
   intrinsic value is an estimate.
3. Why is "value vs growth" a false dichotomy? How do Graham, Fisher, and
   Buffett each support the synthesis?
4. Name Hagstrom's four filters. Which three concern quality-and-growth, and
   which one concerns price?
5. Apply any three of Fisher's 15 points to a company you use every day. What
   did you learn about it?
6. What is the circle of competence, and what is the first rule of investing
   outside it?
7. What does Buffett mean by "price is what you pay, value is what you get"?
   Where does the margin of safety live in that sentence?
8. How does reading Buffett's own letters change how you would evaluate a
   Buffett-style investment thesis?
