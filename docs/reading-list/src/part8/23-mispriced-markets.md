## What this topic is about

Let's start with the honest confession that the source README of this collection
makes about these books: they are **not part of the trading path**. They were
included in the library for completeness, not because either will teach you to
read a balance sheet, size a position, or execute an order. And yet — the
surprise is that both are by one author, Michael Lewis, and both are, in
disguise, finance books.

*Moneyball* is literally a value-investing case study wearing a baseball
uniform: statistics used to find assets the market's heuristics misprice.
*The Blind Side* is a study in spotting undervalued potential and the power of
position. Neither one contains a single ratio, but both train the exact muscle
that Chapters 2 and 4 of this book train: *find where the market's model is
wrong, buy there, and wait for re-rating.*

Where does this sit in the macro→micro path? On the far side of the market —
it feeds nothing that comes later except the reader's own pattern recognition.
The central question these books answer is not "how do markets work" but "how
does a mispricing come to exist at all."

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. Mispricing is a search problem, and statistics is the search tool.**
*Moneyball* shows a market failing because its heuristics are wrong. Baseball
scouts valued players by tradition and physical look; Billy Beane's Oakland A's
valued them by measurable performance — on-base percentage in particular — and
bought what the scouts' heuristics undervalued. This is the identical logic of
value investing: find an asset whose price is set by a stale or lazy model, buy
it cheaply, and let the market eventually re-rate it. If you have read Chapter 2's
value masters, you have read *Moneyball*'s thesis already; you just read it in
dollar signs instead of batting statistics.

**2. Value is often positional — it lives in where an asset stands, not only in
what it is.** *The Blind Side* makes this concrete: the left tackle's job is to
protect the quarterback's blind side, and the rise of the passing game turned
that role from an undervalued position into one of the most valuable jobs on the
field. The same asset, standing in a different role, commands a different price.
The investing lesson is to ask not only "what is this worth?" but "who is exposed
if this fails, and how much is that protection worth to them?"

**3. Both books are the same argument at two scales.** One market (baseball
players) where a heuristic is wrong and a small operator exploits it; one market
(players as physical assets) where the *position's* economics change and the
market is slow to reprice. Together they give you the two things to look for
whenever you hunt for an edge: a wrong model, and a changing job description.

## Book-by-book annotations

### Moneyball — Michael Lewis (2004, ★ 4.6)

The most useful "not a trading book" ever put on an investor's shelf. Lewis tells
the story of Billy Beane, general manager of the small-budget Oakland Athletics,
who competed with baseball's richest clubs by ignoring the scouting heuristics
everyone else trusted and buying players the statistics said were underpriced.
The financial logic — find where the market's model is wrong, buy there, wait for
re-rating — is indistinguishable from value investing, which is why this book
shows up on finance reading lists far more often than its cover suggests.

- **Read it for:** the classic demonstration of a broken pricing heuristic; the
  on-base-percentage story as a mispricing case study; how a small operator
  competes with well-funded incumbents; the single clearest explanation of "the
  market can be wrong and you can exploit it."
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: Moneyball, updated ed., 2012. Ask your AI
  study companion to expand this section from its training data.

### The Blind Side — Michael Lewis (2007, ★ 4.4)

The story of Michael Oher, a homeless teenager taken in by a Tennessee family and
developed into an NFL left tackle — and the analysis Lewis wraps around it: the
position of left tackle exists to protect the quarterback's blind side, and
changes in how the game was played turned a position the market had long
underpaid into one of the most valuable on the field. It is a book about
undervalued potential and about how much a role's price depends on its position.

- **Read it for:** the clearest real-world example of "positional advantage" —
  value created by where you stand; how a market can persistently misprice a role
  it measures poorly; the craft of telling a human story and a market story in
  the same book.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.
