# 24 · Macroeconomics, Fiction & Leisure

> Part VIII · Chapter 24 · Books: 3 · Topic level: beyond the market · Optional

## What this topic is about

The source README of this collection is honest about the books in Part VIII:
they are **not part of the trading path** — they were included for
completeness. This chapter holds the three that are furthest from the
curriculum, and it also holds the most important structural lesson in the
book's final part.

*Boomerang* is the macro version of Chapter 3's leverage risk, played out on
national balance sheets. *The New New Thing* is about how brand-new assets get
priced before anyone has numbers for them. *The Broker* is pure fiction — a
novel — and it earns its place as the book's honest example of reading for
pleasure.

A trading career is not sustained by the books that feed it — it is sustained by
the person who keeps showing up to read them. This chapter is the after-hours
shelf: books you read with your guard down, for no testable reason. That is not
an excuse; it is a strategy. Rest and wide reading are part of sustainable
trading, the same way recovery days are part of a training plan.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. Leverage is the same risk at every scale — including national.** *Boomerang*
travels through Iceland, Ireland, Greece and the rest of Europe after 2008 and
finds the same pattern Chapter 3 describes at the household and portfolio level:
borrow cheaply, buy rising assets, discover the assets were never worth the debt
they secured. A country's balance sheet is just a trader's margin account written
larger. Read *Boomerang* when Chapter 3's risk material feels too abstract, and
it will make margin calls and leverage ratios feel very concrete indeed.

**2. New assets are priced by narrative before they are priced by numbers.**
*The New New Thing* follows serial entrepreneur Jim Clark through Silicon
Graphics, Netscape, and the birth of the internet boom — a cycle in which a
genuinely new asset class gets a valuation before anyone has data to justify it.
That is precisely the moment the earlier chapters' discipline matters most: when
there is no earnings history, the price is a story, and stories are what
*Extraordinary Popular Delusions* (Chapter 1) warned you about.

**3. Some books are for rest, and rest is part of the job.** *The Broker* is a
novel. It teaches no market lesson, and it is not supposed to. Its inclusion is
the collection's final piece of pedagogy: a sustainable trader is a whole human
being, and reading for pleasure is a skill with a real payoff — it is the
counterweight that lets the serious books keep their edge.

## Book-by-book annotations

### Boomerang — Michael Lewis (2012, ★ 4.3)

A tour of the aftermath of the 2008 crisis, visiting Iceland, Ireland, Greece,
Germany, and California. Lewis reads each country's financial collapse as a
national character study — the Icelandic bankers who borrowed in a currency they
could not print, the Irish property bubble built on construction, the Greek state
that never taxed enough to pay for itself. Written as travel comedy, it carries a
serious cargo: leverage, applied at the scale of a whole nation's balance sheet,
is the same risk a margin trader runs, just slower to resolve.

- **Read it for:** national-balance-sheet leverage as Chapter 3's risk written
  large; the mechanics of a country running out of other people's money; the
  uncomfortable reminder that "diversified across countries" is still correlated
  when the shock is a debt crisis; proof that the macro view can be funny and
  fatal at once.

### The New New Thing — Michael Lewis (2014, ★ 4.1)

A portrait of Jim Clark, the serial entrepreneur behind Silicon Graphics, the
Netscape browser, and a string of companies after them — and, through him, the
anatomy of an innovation cycle. Clark's genius is for arriving at a genuinely new
thing just before anyone else sees it, and the book is fascinated by the moment
when such a new asset receives its first valuation: long before there is revenue
or earnings to justify it, the price is set by narrative. For the investor, that
is both the opportunity and the trap.

- **Read it for:** how new assets get priced before the numbers exist; the
  psychology and timing of an innovation cycle (Silicon Graphics → Netscape →
  the internet boom); the entrepreneur's view of value creation, from the side of
  the table most investors never see.

### The Broker — John Grisham (2005, ★ 4.1)

Pure fiction, and the chapter's honesty checkpoint. A Washington power broker,
pardoned by a lame-duck president, is hidden in Bologna with a new identity and
an Italian tutor — while intelligence agencies around the world close in on him.
It is a thriller, not a parable. Its role here is to be the book you are allowed
to enjoy without extracting a lesson, the explicit proof that this shelf is rest,
not curriculum.

- **Read it for:** the pleasure of a well-paced story with no note-taking
  required; a working demonstration that a long reading career needs lighter
  evenings; the permission, from the collection itself, to read something for fun.

## Reading order

1. **Boomerang** — the macro view of leverage (start here).
2. **The New New Thing** — the innovation cycle, for whenever you meet a new asset.
3. **The Broker** — save it for a weekend, and enjoy it.
