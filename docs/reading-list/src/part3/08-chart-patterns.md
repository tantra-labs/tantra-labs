# 8 · Chart Patterns

> Part III · Chapter 8 · Books: 4 · Topic level: chart ▸ micro

## What this topic is about

[Chapter 7](07-candlesticks.md) gave you the grammar — what a single candle
says about a single session. This chapter teaches the sentences: multi-bar
shapes, called **chart patterns**, that traders believe mark either a pause
within a trend or a turning point at the end of one. Patterns are the oldest
tools in technical analysis, and also the most argued-about.

The central question this chapter answers is the one pattern books rarely agree
on: *do these shapes actually repeat, and can you trade them profitably?* The
four books here split into two camps, and studying both camps is the point.
Thomas Bulkowski's two titles treat patterns as statistical objects — he
measured thousands of real historical patterns and published each pattern's
average performance and failure rate. The other two treat patterns as visual
signals to be recognized on the chart and combined with Fibonacci levels and
pivot points for a higher-probability entry.

The shared vocabulary is small: **continuation** versus **reversal** patterns,
the **breakout** (the price move that completes a pattern), the **measured
move** (a price target derived from the pattern's size), **failure rate** (how
often a breakout fails), and **volume confirmation**. This chapter sits
between candlesticks and [Volume & Price Action](09-volume-price-action.md) —
the next chapter explains *why* the volume that accompanies these breakouts
matters so much. None of this is specific to any market: the same flags and
head-and-shoulders shapes appear on NSE/BSE charts as on US charts.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. Sort every pattern into continuation or reversal first.**
A **continuation pattern** means the crowd is pausing and the trend is likely to
resume; a **reversal pattern** means the trend is exhausting and likely to turn.
The classic continuation set: **flags** and **pennants** (brief, tight
consolidations after a sharp move, shaped like a parallelogram or a small
symmetrical triangle), **triangles** (**ascending** — flat top, rising lower
edge; **descending** — flat bottom, falling upper edge; **symmetrical** — both
edges converging), **rectangles** (price bounded between two horizontal
levels), and **wedges** (both edges converging). The classic reversal set:
**head-and-shoulders** and its inverse, **double** and **triple tops and
bottoms**, and the slow **rounding top and bottom**. Every book here agrees
that you must name the pattern *before* it completes — the tradeable event is
the breakout.

**2. Bulkowski's statistics-first method.**
The *Encyclopedia* is the only book here that answers "do patterns work?" with
data instead of anecdotes. Bulkowski collects thousands of real historical
patterns, defines each one precisely, then measures what happens after the
breakout: the average rise or decline, the **failure rate** (patterns that
break out and then reverse before reaching their target), how often price
**pulls back** or "throws back" to the breakout level, and what volume did on
breakout day. His honest tables show that every pattern fails a meaningful
share of the time — which is exactly why patterns must be traded with stops,
never held on faith.

**3. The measured move gives you a price target.**
Patterns are not just shapes; their size implies a target. The three standard
rules, as ASCII math:

```text
Head-and-shoulders top:
  pattern height = head high - neckline level
  price target   = neckline breakout price - pattern height

Flag / pennant:
  pole length   = move from trend start to the flag
  price target  = flag breakout price + pole length   (up-flag)

Triangle:
  width         = widest part of the triangle
  price target  = breakout price + width (up) or - width (down)
```

Targets are probabilities, not promises — price reaches the measured move more
often when volume confirms the breakout, but the target is where you *take*
profit and *reassess*, not a guarantee.

**4. Trade the breakout, not the pattern.**
Until price closes through the pattern's boundary — the neckline, trendline, or
range edge — the pattern is a hypothesis. The trade is the **breakout**: the
move that confirms the crowd has committed. This is why Bulkowski's statistics
measure *breakout* performance and failure rates: the pattern earns nothing
until price acts. Expect the frequent **throwback or pullback** to the breakout
level before the trend resumes, and treat a low-volume breakout — price moving
through a boundary without conviction — as the most failure-prone of all.

**5. Volume is the breakout's witness.**
Inside flags, pennants, and triangles, volume typically **contracts** as the
pattern builds; at the breakout it should **expand**. Rising volume on the
breakout confirms that real money is pushing price through the boundary;
falling volume means the move is probably a fake-out. This single rule is the
bridge to [Chapter 9](09-volume-price-action.md), which builds an entire method
on it.

**6. Pesavento's confluence: pattern + Fibonacci + pivot.**
*Trade What You See* adds a second, independent layer of evidence: Fibonacci
retracements (**0.382, 0.500, 0.618**) and extensions (**1.272, 1.618**),
classic harmonic structures such as **AB = CD**, and **pivot levels**. The
idea of **confluence** is that a pattern breakout occurring *at* a Fibonacci
level *and* a pivot is a better trade than the same pattern alone — two or
three independent camps of traders are agreeing on the same price zone.

**7. Bulkowski's core message: an edge, not a guarantee.**
Every pattern in the *Encyclopedia* has a *positive but imperfect* statistical
edge. Failure rates are nonzero for every single pattern, which is why the
whole method — in all four books — is the same: identify the pattern, wait for
the breakout, confirm with volume, place the stop beyond the pattern's
invalidating level, and take profit at the measured move. The edge survives
only when the losses from the failures are kept small.

## Book-by-book annotations

### Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns — Thomas N. Bulkowski (2005, ★ 4.3)

The heavyweight and the only truly statistical book in this chapter. Bulkowski
presents more than fifty named patterns, each with its measured performance,
failure rate, breakout behavior, throwbacks and pullbacks, and volume
signatures — a reference you consult by pattern, not a book you read start to
finish.

- **Read it for:** per-pattern statistics (performance, failure rate); the
  measured-move rules; breakout and pullback behavior; the honest tables that
  prove no pattern is a sure thing.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 3rd ed., 2016; ask your AI study companion
  to expand these notes from its training data.

### Trade What You See — Larry Pesavento & Leslie Jouflas (2007, ★ 4.0)

The harmony book. Pesavento teaches you to "trade what you see" — to act only
when the chart itself lines up a Fibonacci level, a harmonic shape such as
AB = CD, and a pivot point at the same price. Confluence, not indicators, is
the entry trigger.

- **Read it for:** Fibonacci retracement and extension levels; the AB = CD and
  Gartley-style harmonic structures; pivot-point confluence; real chart
  examples of waiting for the set-up to complete.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Chart Your Way To Profits — author: — (2010, ★ 4.2)

A hands-on charting manual: it takes the pattern vocabulary and shows you how to
apply it to real trading — reading charts, spotting the same formations in
daily practice, and acting on them with a workable plan. The catalog does not
verify this book's author, so the author is listed as "—" here rather than
guessed.

- **Read it for:** practical, chart-by-chart pattern recognition; applying the
  pattern vocabulary to live charts; a trader's working method for entries and
  exits; the confidence that comes from seeing patterns repeatedly.
- **Model refresh:** The catalog lists the author as "—" (unverified). Edition
  here appears current; your AI companion can still expand these notes from its
  training data.

### Visual Guide to Chart Patterns — Thomas N. Bulkowski (2012, ★ 4.0)

Bulkowski's illustrated companion to the *Encyclopedia*: page after page of
real charts showing each pattern's anatomy, where the breakout happens, what a
pullback looks like, and what failure looks like. The fastest way to build the
visual recognition the statistics depend on.

- **Read it for:** visual pattern identification; pattern anatomy drawn out;
  sample entry and exit zones on real charts; a quick visual pass before the
  statistics-heavy *Encyclopedia*.
- **Model refresh:** A later edition may exist — 2nd ed., 2016 (suspected). Ask
  your AI study companion to check its training data for any edition newer than
  2012.

## Reading order

1. **Visual Guide to Chart Patterns** — build visual recognition first; the
   fastest read in the chapter.
2. **Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns** — add the statistics and failure rates;
   the depth read.
3. **Chart Your Way To Profits** — practice applying the patterns to real
   charts.
4. **Trade What You See** — add Fibonacci and pivot confluence for entries.

## Self-test questions

1. What separates a continuation pattern from a reversal pattern, and what is
   the tradeable event in both cases?
2. Name two continuation and two reversal patterns, and describe a
   head-and-shoulders top with its neckline, head, and shoulders in words or
   ASCII.
3. How does Bulkowski measure a pattern's performance, and why is the failure
   rate the number that keeps you disciplined?
4. Given a head high of 120 and a neckline breakout at 100, compute the
   measured-move target.
5. What does expanding volume at a breakout suggest, and what does a low-volume
   breakout suggest?
6. Why do you trade the breakout rather than the pattern itself?
7. What is "confluence" in Pesavento's method, and which three elements are
   combined at a single price?
8. Why do stops and measured targets matter even for a pattern with a positive
   statistical edge?
