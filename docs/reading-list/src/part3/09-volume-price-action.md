# 9 · Volume & Price Action

> Part III · Chapter 9 · Books: 4 · Topic level: chart ▸ micro

## What this topic is about

[Chapter 8](08-chart-patterns.md) taught the shapes; this chapter strips most of
them away and asks you to read the two raw inputs that produce every chart:
**price** and **volume**. The doctrine shared by all four books is that price is
the *leading* variable — it is the actual auction between buyers and sellers —
while every indicator (moving averages, oscillators, and so on) is a *lagging*
transformation of price that can confirm a move but never see one first.

The central question: *can you build a complete discretionary trade from
bar-by-bar price context and volume alone, without an indicator on the chart?*
The books answer yes, then spend hundreds of pages teaching how: how to read the
market bar by bar, how to judge whether volume is confirming or refuting a
move, and how to assemble a trade from a context, a trigger, a stop, and a
target.

The four books are complementary. Adam Grimes supplies the theory and the
science. Anna Coulling supplies the volume interpretation (volume spread
analysis, or **VSA**). *Naked Forex* supplies the discipline of trading a clean
chart. John Person supplies the most mechanical system (pivots plus
candlesticks). The honest theme running through all four: this kind of reading
is **interpretive, not mechanical** — which is exactly why the next part of this
book, [Trading Psychology & Discipline](../part4/10-psychology.md), follows.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. Price leads; indicators lag.**
An indicator is a formula applied to *past* price (and sometimes volume). It can
describe what already happened and confirm a suspected move, but it cannot know
what price will do next. Price action reads the auction directly: where is price
relative to recent swings, and are buyers or sellers in control *right now*?
The synthesis: indicators are optional furniture; price is the room.

**2. Read the market bar by bar (Grimes).**
Grimes's framework rests on four dimensions:

- **Structure** — the pattern of swing highs and lows and the market's phases
  (accumulation, markup, distribution, markdown);
- **Trend** — are swings making progress in a direction, or stalling?
- **Momentum** — how do recent bars compare with the bars before them?
- **Trade management** — once you are in, what is the stop, the target, and the
  invalidation?

Every decision in this chapter begins by placing the current bar in its
structural context before acting on it.

**3. Volume spread analysis (VSA): effort versus result.**
VSA reads each bar as a small battle between supply and demand. The core idea:

```text
Effort vs result:
  effort  = the bar's volume
  result  = the bar's price range and close position

  Large effort, small result (huge volume, narrow range)  = absorption:
     someone is soaking up supply (or demand) — a warning of a turn.
  Down-bar, shrinking volume, small range near the lows   = "no supply":
     sellers are exhausted — a possible bottom.
  Up-bar, tiny volume                                     = "no demand":
     buyers are absent — the rally is suspect.
```

VSA adds a narrative layer called the **"smart money" footprint**: professionals
accumulate and distribute quietly, and wide-range **climax** bars mark the
exhaustion ends of moves. These ideas descend from Wyckoff's market phases and
give volume a story, not just a count.

**4. Volume confirms or refutes price.**
This is the chapter's single most practical rule: a price move is only as
strong as the volume behind it. A rally on shrinking volume is weak — there is
no new demand, so it is likely to fail. A breakout on expanding volume is real.
A decline on shrinking volume means selling is drying up. Price says *what*
happened; volume says *whether anyone believes it*.

**5. Multi-timeframe confluence.**
The books agree that a lower-timeframe signal should be read against a
higher-timeframe context: the daily chart sets the direction bias, the intraday
chart supplies the trigger. A long setup on the daily is far more credible if
the weekly structure is still an uptrend — confluence across timeframes filters
out the signals that fight the larger move.

**6. Build a discretionary trade from price and volume alone.**
Stripped of indicators, the trade has five parts:

```text
A minimal price+volume trade:
  1. Higher-timeframe context (structure / trend)  -> direction bias
  2. A level (support, resistance, prior swing)    -> where to act
  3. A trigger bar (strong close on volume)        -> when to act
  4. Invalidation (close beyond the setup bar)     -> where the idea is wrong
  5. Target (prior level or measured move)         -> where to take profit
```

No indicator is required at any step; the discipline is in waiting for all five
to line up.

**7. The honest limits.**
VSA and price action are interpretive, not mechanical: two careful readers can
disagree on whether a bar is absorption or simple profit-taking, and the
"smart money" is an inference, not something visible on the chart. Because the
method leans on judgment, it leans on discipline — the book's own reason for
sending you to the psychology chapter next. The same reading applies to Nifty
and USD/INR charts as to any global market.

## Book-by-book annotations

### A Complete Guide to Technical Trading Tactics — John L. Person (2004, ★ 3.5)

The most rule-based book in the chapter. Person's method builds intraday
support and resistance from the prior day's high, low, and close (his
"Person's Pivots"), then combines those levels with candle signals and a short
indicator set into a repeatable daily trading plan.

- **Read it for:** the pivot-point calculations; combining pivots with
  candlesticks; a structured, repeatable intraday plan; concrete entry, stop,
  and target tactics around pivots.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Naked Forex — Alex Nekritin & Walter Peters (2012, ★ 4.2)

The price-action manifesto: strip the charts down to price alone and trade
simple, recognizable bar patterns — the **inside bar** being the best known —
in confluence with the surrounding structure. Written for forex, but the
clean-chart logic carries over directly to stocks and indices.

- **Read it for:** the discipline of a chart with no indicators; the inside-bar
  setup and confluence rules; simple risk-defined entries; the psychological
  case for trusting what you see over what an indicator says.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### The Art and Science of Technical Analysis — Adam Grimes (2012, ★ 4.6)

The deepest book in the chapter and the one that turns price action into a
discipline. Grimes builds from the market as an auction up to a complete
framework — market structure and phases, bar-by-bar price action, the
probabilities and expectancy that make an edge real, position sizing, and the
trader's own psychology.

- **Read it for:** market structure and the four phases; bar-by-bar price
  action; expectancy and position sizing; how to design and test a strategy;
  the honest chapter on the trader's mind.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### A Complete Guide To Volume Price Analysis — Anna Coulling (2013, ★ 4.5)

VSA made readable. Coulling treats volume as the cause and price as the effect,
walks you through effort-versus-result, no-supply and no-demand bars,
absorption, and climaxes, and shows each idea on real charts across stocks,
forex, and futures.

- **Read it for:** the effort-versus-result framework; no-supply and no-demand
  bars; absorption and the "smart money" view; practical chart-by-chart VSA
  walk-throughs.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

## Reading order

1. **Naked Forex** — the fastest on-ramp: a clean chart and one pattern.
2. **The Art and Science of Technical Analysis** — the full framework and the
   science; the depth read.
3. **A Complete Guide To Volume Price Analysis** — add volume and VSA on top of
   the price reading.
4. **A Complete Guide to Technical Trading Tactics** — the most mechanical
   system, best after you can already read bars.

## Self-test questions

1. Why is price called the leading variable and every indicator a lagging one?
2. Name Grimes's four reading dimensions for a bar-by-bar decision.
3. Explain "effort vs result" and give one absorption example.
4. What is a "no-supply" bar, and what does it imply about the market?
5. Why should a price rise on falling volume be treated with suspicion?
6. How does a higher timeframe change the meaning of a lower-timeframe trigger?
7. Build a complete discretionary trade from price and volume alone — context,
   level, trigger, invalidation, and target — without naming a single
   indicator.

   does that imply for the discipline needed to trade it?
