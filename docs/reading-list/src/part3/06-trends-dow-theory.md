# 6 · Trends, Dow Theory & Chart Construction

> Part III · Chapter 6 · Books: 8 · Topic level: macro ▸ chart

## What this topic is about

Every technical-analysis book in this library stands on one claim: price history
has structure that repeats, and the most important structure is the **trend**.
This chapter is the macro entry to the chart-reading half of the book — the point
where we stop asking "what is the market" (Parts I–II) and start asking "what is
the chart telling me". Dow Theory is where that question was first answered
systematically, and everything in [Chapter 7](07-candlesticks.md) and [Chapter
8](08-chart-patterns.md) is a refinement of its vocabulary.

The central question the eight books answer: can a **trend** be defined
objectively — so that two independent chart readers would draw the same
trendline — and does respecting that definition beat guessing? The shared
vocabulary they assume is thin: a price series over time, a bar, a level.
Everything else — trend, support, resistance, trendline, channel, confirmation —
they teach from first principles.

The intellectual lineage of this topic is unusually clear, and the books here
are aware of it. Charles Dow's editorials in the *Wall Street Journal* at the
turn of the twentieth century; codification by Hamilton and later Rhea; Richard
Schabacker's 1932 classic; Edwards and Magee's 1948 reference; and the modern
textbooks of Murphy and Kirkpatrick–Dahlquist. Because of that lineage the
chapter splits naturally into three layers: the **textbook layer** (Murphy,
Kirkpatrick & Dahlquist), **the classics** (Schabacker, Edwards & Magee), and
the **beginner on-ramp** (For Dummies, The Visual Investor).

Finally, the chapter does the unglamorous work everything else depends on:
constructing the bar chart itself — OHLC bars, time scale, volume panel — from
nothing. You cannot judge a trendline, a support level, a candle, or a pattern
until you can read the raw bar, so the construction step comes first in the
synthesis below.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. Dow Theory in six tenets.** Every modern textbook restates Dow's original
rules, and they are the skeleton of this chapter:

1. **The averages discount everything.** All known information — earnings, news,
   sentiment — is already in the price.
2. **There are three trends at once.** A *primary* trend (months to years), a
   *secondary* trend (weeks to months, a correction against the primary), and a
   *minor* trend (days).
3. **A primary trend has three phases.** In a bull market: *accumulation*
   (informed buyers), *public participation* (the crowd, the markup), and
   *distribution* (informed sellers feeding the crowd).
4. **The averages must confirm each other.** Dow used the Industrial and Rail
   averages (today: Industrials and Transports). A new high or low must be
   confirmed by both; a divergence is a warning.
5. **Volume confirms the trend.** Volume should expand in the direction of the
   primary trend.
6. **Trends persist until definite reversal signals.** Assume the trend is in
   force until it visibly ends — do not try to catch turns early.

**2. The working definition of a trend.** A trend is a sequence of price
reactions: an *uptrend* makes higher highs and higher lows; a *downtrend* makes
lower highs and lower lows. The first failure — a lower low in an uptrend, a
higher high in a downtrend — *suspends* the trend's presumption. This is
Edwards & Magee's core lesson, and the single most useful definition in the
chapter.

**3. Support and resistance.** *Support* is a price zone where buying has
repeatedly entered; *resistance* is a zone where selling has repeatedly entered.
The discipline every book teaches: when a support level is broken it
**becomes** resistance, and when resistance is broken it becomes support (the
"polarity" or role-reversal rule). Levels matter because they are where the
market's memory lives.

**4. Trendlines and channels.** A trendline connects successive reaction lows
(an uptrend) or reaction highs (a downtrend); a parallel line through the
opposite reactions turns it into a **channel**. A close *through* the trendline
is an early warning, not a verdict — the verdict comes from the price-action
sequence of tenet 2 and the volume of tenet 5.

**5. Constructing the bar chart from first principles.** Each time period
(day, week, month) becomes one bar spanning the period's high and low, with
small ticks for the open and close:

```text
   high  ──●
            │        ← upper range of the bar
   open ───┤        ← left tick = open of the period
            │
   close ──┤        ← right tick = close of the period
            │        ← lower range of the bar
   low   ──●
```

Read left to right on a time axis; add a second panel underneath for volume;
choose the bar's time scale so that 40–100 bars tell the story you are studying.
A **weekly bar** smooths the minor trend; a **daily bar** reveals it.

**6. Volume as confirmation.** Volume measures conviction. A rally on rising
volume is more trustworthy than the same rally on shrinking volume; a rally that
cannot pull volume in is a candidate for a secondary reaction. Dow's principle
of confirmation is applied in modern form: judge the price move and the volume
move together, never price alone.

**7. Layering the library.** Read the **on-ramp** layer for fluency, the
**textbook** layer for completeness, and the **classics** for the original
statement. The classics are not museum pieces — Schabacker and Edwards & Magee
define the pattern vocabulary that the textbooks summarise, and their Dow-era
examples remain legible on any modern chart, NSE or NYSE.

## Book-by-book annotations

### Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets — John J. Murphy (1999, ★ 4.6)

The master textbook and the natural spine of this chapter. Murphy rewrote and
expanded his earlier futures-market book into a complete tour: Dow Theory, chart
construction, trends, patterns, indicators, and — uniquely — intermarket
analysis. The one volume that covers every topic in the study synthesis above.

- **Read it for:** the cleanest modern statement of Dow Theory; trendlines and
  channels done precisely; the intermarket section (stocks vs bonds vs
  commodities vs currencies); the "trend first, then signal" workflow.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Technical Analysis Explained — Martin J. Pring (2002, ★ 4.1)

The encyclopedic companion. Where Murphy is a tour, Pring is a reference desk:
Dow Theory in historical depth, cycle analysis, breadth and momentum indicators,
and how institutional analysts structure their work. The book for the reader who
wants to know *why* as well as *what*.

- **Read it for:** the history and formal statement of Dow Theory; business and
  price cycles; breadth and momentum indicators; the professional workflow of
  "top-down" analysis.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 5th ed., 2014; ask your AI study companion
  to expand these notes from its training data.

### Technical Analysis and Stock Market Profits — Richard W. Schabacker (2005 printing, ★ 4.4)

The 1932 classic — first published at the bottom of the Depression market, which
is exactly why its charts are so instructive. Schabacker taught the language of
reversals and continuations that Edwards and Magee later systematised. Reading
him is watching the canon being written.

- **Read it for:** the original statement of the pattern vocabulary; a 1930s
  market full of textbook reversals; the historical bridge between Dow's
  editorials and the modern textbooks; perspective on how little the core
  questions have changed.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Technical Analysis of Stock Trends — Robert D. Edwards & John Magee (2007, ★ 4.1)

The classic reference, first published in 1948 and still the citation for the
field's definitions. The book every other technical-analysis author is
answering. Its chapters on trend, support/resistance, and the major reversal and
continuation formations define the terms used throughout this Part.

- **Read it for:** the definitive statement of trend definition and the
  "line/congestion" analysis; the classic reversal and continuation patterns;
  the Dow Theory appendix; the famous "technical analysis is a discipline"
  framing.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 10th ed., 2012 (Bassetti); ask your AI study
  companion to expand these notes from its training data.

### The Visual Investor — John J. Murphy (2009, ★ 4.4)

Murphy's beginner on-ramp: how to *look* at a chart — trend, volume, relative
strength — with the minimum of indicators. Deliberately visual rather than
computational: the discipline of seeing what is on the chart before adding any
math to it.

- **Read it for:** a gentler Murphy entry than the 1999 textbook; visual trend
  and support/resistance reading; relative strength as a practical filter;
  sector rotation and intermarket relationships in plain form.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Technical Analysis — Charles D. Kirkpatrick & Julie R. Dahlquist (2011, ★ 4.1)

The academic treatment. Kirkpatrick and Dahlquist (both associated with the CMT
professional program) give technical analysis the statistics it is usually
denied: formal definitions, tests of trend-following, and the evidence question
every trader should be able to ask — *does this actually work?*

- **Read it for:** the evidence base for trend-following and Dow Theory; formal
  definitions of trend, support, and resistance; the Dow Theory history through
  Rhea; the market-efficiency discussion from the technician's side.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Technical Analysis For Dummies — Barbara Rockefeller (2011, ★ 4.1)

The beginner on-ramp: charts, trends, support and resistance, indicators, and
candlesticks, from a standing start and with risk treated seriously. The fastest
way into the vocabulary when you are new to charts.

- **Read it for:** a step-by-step reading of a bar chart; trend and
  support/resistance basics without jargon; the first pass at indicators; risk
  and position-sizing framed for a beginner.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 3rd ed., 2014 (suspected); ask your AI study
  companion to expand these notes from its training data.

### Technical Analysis of Stock Trends Explained — author — (2013, ★ 3.6)

A study companion whose very title announces its purpose: to explain the
Edwards & Magee classic in plainer language for readers who found the original
dense. The collection does not verify an author for it — shown as "—" — and we
do not guess.

- **Read it for:** a gentler pass over the Edwards & Magee material; worked
  explanations of trend and pattern concepts; a second voice when the classic
  feels heavy.
- **Model refresh:** Author shown as — (not verified in the collection); ask
  your AI companion to confirm the authorship, and to expand these notes from
  its training data.

## Reading order

1. **Technical Analysis For Dummies** or **The Visual Investor** — the fastest
   on-ramp to the vocabulary.
2. **Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets** — the core read; covers the
   whole chapter's material in one pass.
3. **Technical Analysis of Stock Trends** — the classic, for the original
   definitions once the basics are in place.
4. **Technical Analysis Explained** — breadth and cycles, when you want the
   depth layer.
5. **Technical Analysis and Stock Market Profits** — the 1932 original; dip in
   for history and legibility.
6. **Technical Analysis** — the evidence layer, when you want the statistics.
7. **Technical Analysis of Stock Trends Explained** — optional companion for
   the classic.

## Self-test questions

1. List the six tenets of Dow Theory from memory.
2. Define an uptrend in terms of highs and lows. What single price event would
   make you *suspect* it has ended?
3. In Dow Theory, what is the difference between the primary, secondary, and
   minor trends — and which one is the tradable decision?
4. What does "the averages must confirm each other" mean, and what does a
   divergence between the two averages warn of?
5. Draw an OHLC bar and label open, high, low, and close. How would a weekly bar
   differ from a daily bar on the same stock?
6. Support is broken — what does the level become, and why does that matter?
7. A stock rallies to a new high but volume is lower than on the prior high.
   What does the Dow principle of confirmation say about this rally?
8. Per Dow Theory, what must happen *before* you conclude a primary trend has
   reversed rather than merely paused?
