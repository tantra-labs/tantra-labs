# 7 · Candlesticks

> Part III · Chapter 7 · Books: 7 · Topic level: chart ▸ micro

## What this topic is about

Candlesticks are a rendering of the **same four numbers** as the bar chart of
[Chapter 6](06-trends-dow-theory.md) — open, high, low, close — but in a shape
that makes the period's psychology visible at a glance: who won, buyers or
sellers, and by how much. This is the micro step down from chart construction:
from "what a chart is" to "what a single price bar can say."

The history gives the topic its shape. Candlestick charting was developed in the
rice markets of eighteenth-century Osaka and carried into Western practice by
Steve Nison's work in the late 1980s. Nison wrote three of the seven books in
this chapter — the founding text, the sequel, and the workbook — so the study
path the books themselves suggest is: **read Nison first**, then **read Morris**
for the statistics that temper Nison's enthusiasm.

The central question: do single price bars, read in the right context, carry
predictive information? The honest answer every book here converges on is:
patterns have *real but modest* predictive power, and only in context. A hammer
at the bottom of a downtrend is a different object from a hammer at the top of
an uptrend. The pattern is the vocabulary; the trend is the grammar; and a
price-bar shape alone is never a trade.

The chapter closes with John Person's combination system — candlestick signals
fired *at* pivot-point levels — because it shows the micro language of candles
being reattached to the macro levels of support and resistance. Candles tell you
*when*; pivot levels tell you *where*.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. Anatomy: body, shadows, and one question.** Every candle encodes one
question — did the close beat the open? — plus the range of the battle:

```text
   high   ●
          │     ← upper shadow (wick): how far sellers pushed price
   ┌─────────┐
   │  body   │   ← hollow body: close above open (bullish)
   └─────────┘     filled body: close below open (bearish)
          │     ← lower shadow (wick): how far buyers pushed price
   low    ●
```

A **long body** means conviction; long **shadows** mean rejection; a tiny body
means the period was a fight neither side won. Read bodies first, shadows second.

**2. Single-candle patterns.** The one-candle vocabulary:

- **Doji** — open ≈ close: indecision; special forms (long-legged, dragonfly,
  gravestone) sharpen the reading of the market's state.
- **Hammer** — small body at the top, long lower shadow, appearing in a
  downtrend: bullish rejection of lower prices.
- **Hanging man** — the *same shape* as the hammer, appearing in an uptrend:
  bearish, because buyers rejected the low only after a rally.
- **Shooting star** — small body at the bottom, long upper shadow, in an
  uptrend: bearish rejection of higher prices.
- **Marubozu** — no shadows, one clean move: strong, one-sided conviction.
- **Spinning top** — small body with shadows on both sides: uncertainty.

**3. Two-candle patterns.** The second candle is read against the first:

- **Bullish engulfing** — a bearish candle is fully engulfed by a larger bullish
  candle: buyers overwhelmed sellers.
- **Bearish engulfing** — the mirror image.
- **Harami** — a small candle inside the previous candle's body: the trend is
  pausing; a **harami cross** is the doji version.
- **Piercing line** (bullish) and **dark cloud cover** (bearish) — the second
  candle opens below/above the first's close and recovers partway, showing the
  rejection without full engulfing.
- **Tweezers** — matching highs (top) or lows (bottom): a rejection at a
  shared level.

A tiny diagram of the bullish engulfing:

```text
 period 1:   ▄▄▄▄  (bearish, small)
 period 2:   ██████  (bullish, large — swallows period 1's whole range)
```

**4. Three-candle patterns.** Sequences, not pairs:

- **Morning star** — a large bearish candle, a small body (often a doji), then a
  large bullish candle: a three-period reversal at a bottom.
- **Evening star** — the mirror image at a top.
- **Three white soldiers** — three rising bullish candles, each closing near its
  high: sustained buying.
- **Three black crows** — three falling bearish candles: sustained selling.

**5. Context matters more than the pattern.** Nison's own framework is
three-stage: identify the **trend** first, then the **pattern**, then seek
**confirmation** (a following candle, volume, or a level). A pattern against the
prevailing trend is a reversal candidate; the same pattern *with* the trend is a
continuation sign; the identical shape in isolation is nothing. This is the
chapter's single most important lesson and the direct link back to Chapter 6's
trend discipline.

**6. Morris's statistics — the reality check.** Gregory Morris ran the numbers
that most candle books skip: patterns have real but *modest* predictive power.
They beat chance — that is why they are worth learning — but they are far from
certain, they improve when filtered by context, and they must be traded with
stops and position sizing like any other signal. Morris is the antidote to
believing a doji is a trading plan.

**7. Candles + pivots (Person).** Pivot points turn the prior period's prices
into support/resistance levels for the next:

```text
Pivot   P  = (High + Low + Close) / 3
R1         = 2 * P - Low
S1         = 2 * P - High
R2         = P + (High - Low)
S2         = P - (High - Low)
```

Person's system: the pivot gives the *where* (a level), the candle pattern gives
the *when* (a trigger at that level). A candle signal that fires *at* a pivot
level is stronger than either element alone — the micro pattern reattached to the
macro level.

## Book-by-book annotations

### Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques — Steve Nison (2001, ★ 4.5)

The founding text — the book that introduced candlesticks to the Western world
when it first appeared in 1991. Everything is here: the Osaka rice-market
history, the construction rules, the full pattern vocabulary, and — crucially —
how to integrate candles with the bar-chart and indicator analysis of Chapter 6.

- **Read it for:** the complete pattern vocabulary in one place; the history of
  the technique; Nison's context-and-confirmation rules; the integration of
  candles with Western indicators.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Beyond Candlesticks — Steve Nison (1994, ★ 4.2)

Nison's sequel: the patterns and chart types the founding text could not fit,
including Renko, Kagi, and three-line-break charts, and a more institutional
look at applying candles in live markets. Read after the founding text — it
presumes the vocabulary.

- **Read it for:** Renko, Kagi, and three-line-break charts; the additional
  reversal and continuation patterns; an institutional angle on candle
  application; the "candles in the real market" chapters.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### The Candlestick Course — Steve Nison (2003, ★ 4.5)

The workbook version of the same author: structured lessons, pattern quizzes,
and self-tests that force you to *name the pattern* rather than merely recognize
it. The fastest way to internalise the vocabulary — the drilling stage of the
study path.

- **Read it for:** the exercise-driven format; self-tests that build recognition
  speed; a gentler route into the two big Nison books; the emphasis on reading
  patterns in trend context.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Candlestick Charting Explained — Gregory L. Morris (2006, ★ 4.4)

The statistics book and this chapter's reality check. Morris defines each
pattern with precision and then reports what the data actually show: which
patterns perform, which do not, and how much edge they genuinely carry. The
"modest but real" verdict is the correction every enthusiast needs.

- **Read it for:** the statistical evidence on pattern reliability; precise
  pattern definitions; context filters that improve performance; a sober
  framework for using candles without over-trusting them.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Candlestick Charting For Dummies — Russell Rhoads (2008, ★ 4.3)

The beginner on-ramp: candle construction, the basic pattern family, and simple
applications, all with risk discipline properly front-loaded. A friendly first
read if Nison's density feels intimidating.

- **Read it for:** a standing start on candle construction; the first pass over
  the pattern family; combining candles with basic trend analysis; the risk and
  discipline chapters.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Getting Started in Candlestick Charting — Tina Logan (2008, ★ 4.4)

A step-by-step primer from the Wiley "Getting Started" series, with a stronger
emphasis than most on reading candles *within* the prevailing trend rather than
as isolated shapes. More methodical hand-holding than the Dummies entry, and a
useful second voice alongside Nison.

- **Read it for:** the step-by-step structure; the trend-context emphasis;
  chart walk-throughs and exercises; a teacherly voice that complements Nison.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Candlestick and Pivot Point Trading Triggers — John L. Person (2006, ★ 4.0)

The combination system. Person joins candle patterns to pivot-point levels: the
pivot gives the price level, the candle gives the trigger, and the rule is to
wait until both line up. The chapter's demonstration that candle micro-language
composes with the support/resistance macro-language of Chapter 6.

- **Read it for:** pivot-point calculation and use; candle patterns as triggers
  at levels; a complete mechanical framework; the discipline of waiting for
  level *and* pattern to agree.

## Reading order

1. **Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques** — the vocabulary; start here.
2. **The Candlestick Course** — the drills, right after #1.
3. **Candlestick Charting Explained** — the statistics; the reality check.
4. **Candlestick Charting For Dummies** or **Getting Started in Candlestick
   Charting** — a gentler pass if Nison felt heavy.
5. **Beyond Candlesticks** — the advanced layer, once the basics are fluent.
6. **Candlestick and Pivot Point Trading Triggers** — the combination system, to
   reconnect candles with levels.

## Self-test questions

1. Draw a bullish candle and a bearish candle; label open, high, low, close,
   body, and the two shadows.
2. What does a doji tell you, and what does a marubozu tell you?
3. A hammer and a hanging man have the same shape. What decides which name the
   candle receives?
4. Describe the two candles of a bullish engulfing pattern. What must be true of
   the first candle for the signal to be clean?
5. Draw the three-candle sequence of a morning star. Where should it appear for
   it to mean "reversal" rather than "noise"?
6. Why does trend context matter more than the pattern shape itself?
7. According to Morris's statistics, how much predictive power do candlestick
   patterns genuinely have — and what does that imply about position sizing?
8. In Person's system, what does the pivot point contribute and what does the
   candle contribute?
