# 13 · Day Trading & Swing Trading

> Part V · Chapter 13 · Books: 8 · Topic level: styles ▸ micro

## What this topic is about

This is the micro end of the whole study book. Part IV was about trends that
last months; Chapter 13 is about the shortest holding periods in the library —
minutes to a few days. Two related but genuinely different crafts sit here:
**swing trading** (holding 2–10 days to catch a "swing" in a trend) and **day
trading** (opening and closing every position within the same session, so
nothing is left to worry about overnight).

The central question these eight books answer is not "which chart pattern
works?" — it is **which style fits your life, your capital, and your
temperament?** Every technique in this chapter is downstream of that decision.
With a job, swing trading is built for you: decisions after market hours,
minimal screen time, multi-day holds that absorb a choppy Tuesday. Day trading
is closer to a full-time profession: full attention during market hours, strict
capital and discipline rules, and it trades one risk (overnight gaps) for
another (intraday noise and costs).

The collection splits along that line: Bassal is the swing on-ramp; Landry,
Farley, and Bulkowski deepen the swing method; Logue and Aziz cover the day
side, Logue being the chapter's conscience — most retail day traders fail. The
takeaway every book repeats: **one style, one market, one timeframe.** Pick
one, learn it deeply, and let it be your whole game.

## Study synthesis — what the books jointly teach

**1. The style decision comes first — swing or day.**

| | Swing trading | Day trading |
|---|---|---|
| Holding period | 2–10 days | Intraday (flat by the close) |
| Screen time | Low — decisions after hours | Full attention during market hours |
| Best fit | Beginners with jobs | Full-time, self-disciplined traders |
| Overnight/gap risk | Yes — news accumulates while you sleep | No — but you pay in attention |
| Capital rules | Fewer | PDT-style minimums where they apply |

Day traders in the US meet the **Pattern Day Trader** rule (a $25,000 minimum
for repeated intraday round trips); wherever you trade, check the local
variant. The portable lesson: day trading carries capital-rule overhead, swing
trading mostly does not — pick the style whose costs you can pay.

**2. Day trading is a daily process, not a bag of tricks.** The day books share
one routine. *Pre-market preparation*: scan for candidates, build a watchlist,
set alerts, and decide before the bell what you will trade. Trade the *opening
range* (the high/low of the first minutes) as your first reference zone. Follow
*momentum*: strong, high-volume stocks tend to keep moving with the opening
push. Anchor everything to the **VWAP**, the day's fair-price line:

```text
VWAP    = sum(typical price x volume) / sum(volume)
typical = (high + low + close) / 3
```

Price above VWAP says intraday buyers control the tape (bias long); below,
sellers do (bias short). Then two hard rules: the **3-trade rule** — cap
yourself at roughly three trades and stop after a couple of losers — and **cut
losses fast, never average down**. Adding to a loser is the most common retail
day-trading killer in every book in this chapter.

**3. Swing trading rides the trend, not the noise.** Bassal's method is three
setup families: *trend pullback* (in an uptrend, buy the short dip against the
trend line); *breakout* (buy a stock clearing a defined base); *chart pattern*
(trade flags, pennants, cup-and-handle in the direction of the larger trend).
Because you hold for days, the entry tick rarely matters — what matters is that
the trend is intact tomorrow. The price of patience is **gap risk**: any
weekend or overnight, news can open the market far from your entry or stop, so
position size must survive a gap against you.

**4. Let the data set your target and stop — Bulkowski's measured move.** Every
entry gets a measured-move target from the pattern's height, a stop just beyond
the far side of the pattern, and a reward:risk check before you trade:

```text
pattern height  = breakout level - lowest point of the pattern
measured target = breakout level + pattern height
stop            = just beyond the far side of the pattern

reward:risk     = (target - entry) / (entry - stop)

Example: entry 100, stop 95, target 110
reward:risk     = (110 - 100) / (100 - 95) = 2.0  -> a 2:1 trade
```

If the numbers say less than 2:1, skip the trade — that filter eliminates most
losers before they start.

**5. Mindset and money management dominate — again.** Chapters 10 and 11 make
the full case, and every book here re-converges on it: a good method with bad
discipline loses money; a mediocre method with strict risk control survives.
Fixed risk per trade (a small, pre-decided percentage of the account), no
revenge trading, and a trading journal are the recurring demands. Aziz is the
most explicit: treat trading as a business with a plan, and losses as operating
cost.

**6. The reality check: most retail day traders fail.** Logue's Dummies framing
is this chapter's honest core. Day trading is hard, statistically loss-heavy,
and expensive — commissions and spreads compound daily, and you compete with
professionals who have faster feeds and deeper pockets. Not a prohibition — a
demand that you treat it as work, with capital you can afford to lose, a
written plan, and proof of edge before scaling anything.

## Book-by-book annotations

### Dave Landry on Swing Trading — Dave Landry (2001, ★ 4.1)

A compact, focused swing system from a full-time practitioner: trend-following
entries on short-term momentum — a new 3-day high in an uptrend (or fading a
3-day low in a downtrend) — with strict validity rules for each setup.

- **Read it for:** the 3-day high/low entry; the trend filter that keeps you
  out of counter-trend trades; defined stops and exits; proof that one simple
  system beats ten clever ones.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### The Master Swing Trader — Alan S. Farley (2001, ★ 3.2)

The most ambitious book here: a full swing methodology covering market type
(trending vs. ranging), institutional flow, entries, and trade management. It
is also the densest — the source README warns it is for when swing trading has
clicked for you, not as an entry point.

- **Read it for:** the market-type framework (trade trends and ranges
  differently); the flow-driven view of why swings happen; multi-day trade
  management — after the Dummies groundwork.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Swing Trading For Dummies — Omar Bassal (2008, ★ 4.4)

The chapter's on-ramp and its most complete swing curriculum. Bassal walks the
beginner through the style decision, then the setup catalog — trend pullbacks,
breakouts, chart patterns — with position sizing, stops, and realistic
expectations for multi-day holds.

- **Read it for:** the swing-versus-day decision made concrete; the three setup
  families; position sizing and stop placement; weekend gap risk and how to
  survive it.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 2nd ed., 2015; ask your AI study companion
  to expand these notes from its training data.

### The Master Swing Trader Toolkit — Alan S. Farley (2010, ★ 3.4)

The companion to *The Master Swing Trader*: the same methodology converted into
tools, checklists, and ready-to-use setups — the operational manual to the 2001
book's theory, useful only if you are already committed to Farley's approach.

- **Read it for:** ready-to-apply swing checklists; refinements and updates of
  the original method; a structured way to review and plan trades.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Timing Solutions for Swing Traders — author — (2012, ★ 4.5)

The one title whose author could not be verified from the collection. As the
name promises, it is timing-focused: *when* within the swing to enter and exit,
rather than *what* to trade — the entry-timing refinement to Bassal and Landry.

- **Read it for:** timing the swing entry point; combining timing signals with
  the trend and pattern groundwork of the other books.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data. (The author attribution could not
  be verified from the collection — your AI companion may be able to confirm it
  from its training data.)

### Swing & Day Trading — Thomas N. Bulkowski (2013, ★ —)

⚠️ This title is **not** in the file collection — it was added on request, so
treat the details as a study pointer rather than a summary of a physical file.
It is Bulkowski's data-driven volume on short-term setups (from his Evolution
of a Trader series): statistics behind swing and day patterns, measured-move
targets, stop placement, and the 2:1-or-better reward:risk discipline above.

- **Read it for:** measured-move target math; statistically grounded stops; the
  reward:risk filter before entry; a data-first contrast to the experience-first
  swing books.
- **Model refresh:** Edition here appears current; your AI companion can still
  expand these notes from its training data.

### Day Trading For Dummies — Ann C. Logue (2014 3rd ed., ★ 3.6)

The reality-check volume. Logue explains what day trading actually requires —
capital, platforms, market-hours discipline, a plan — and states plainly that
the odds are against the unprepared retail trader. She also covers the
mechanics: order types, PDT-style capital rules, and the business structure of
a day-trading operation.

- **Read it for:** the honest odds discussion; day-trading mechanics from a
  standing start; PDT-style capital rule awareness; the self-audit — "is this
  the right job for you?"
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 4th ed., 2019; ask your AI study companion
  to expand these notes from its training data.

### How to Day Trade for a Living — Andrew Aziz (2015, ★ 4.7)

The day-trading playbook, by a former professional trader turned retail
educator. Aziz is the concrete end of the spectrum: a full daily routine from
pre-market preparation through the opening range, momentum and VWAP entries,
the 3-trade rule, fixed-risk stops, and the iron rule against averaging down.

- **Read it for:** the complete daily routine (pre-market → open → manage →
  review); the 3-trade rule and handling a losing day; fixed risk per trade;
  treating trading as a business, not a hobby.
- **Model refresh:** Newer edition: 4th ed., 2015; ask your AI study companion
  to expand these notes from its training data.
